About the simulation

This started as a small physics experiment for my portfolio: I wanted the field to react to the dog rather than just play a fixed animation. The result is not meant to be a physically complete simulation. Instead, I picked a few effects that are noticeable while walking through grass, gave them simple dynamics, and tried to keep the whole thing cheap enough to run smoothly in a browser.

Dog movement

The dog’s motion is controlled by acceleration rather than setting its position directly. At each physics step, I find the vector from the dog to its target and use the distance to determine how strongly it should accelerate. Farther targets make it speed up; as it gets close, the acceleration falls off and a small dead zone lets it come to rest instead of constantly correcting around the cursor. Velocity is also capped, so a click on the other side of the screen does not produce an unrealistic jump in speed.

That gives the cursor a little slack, almost like an invisible leash. It also means the dog’s velocity is useful elsewhere in the simulation: running quickly through a patch of grass can produce a larger disturbance than slowly walking through the same patch.

Grass as damped springs

I originally considered treating the grass as particles, but that would spend a lot of computation on motion that is barely visible at this scale. Instead, every tuft stays fixed at its root and only its visual bend is simulated. When the dog passes through a tuft, it adds bend velocity in the direction of contact. Afterward, the tuft behaves like a damped spring and returns to its resting position.

For a bend x with bend velocity v, the restoring acceleration is a = −k x − c v. The −k x term pulls the grass back toward its upright position, while −c v removes energy so it settles instead of oscillating forever. Increasing k makes the grass feel stiffer; increasing c makes it settle faster.

This is the standard spring-and-damper model described in Glenn Fiedler’s Spring Physics ↗. The simplification is where I apply it: one spring represents the visible deformation of an entire tuft rather than simulating every blade independently.

Loose grass and particles

The bits kicked into the air are a separate particle system. Each has a position and velocity, with gravity pulling it downward and drag gradually reducing its speed. Particles have short lifetimes and fade once they settle. Sitting produces a larger local impulse, which is why it kicks up a more noticeable burst than ordinary walking.

I deliberately left out particle-particle collisions, airflow, and detailed leaf rotation or deformation. Those would make the model more physically complete, but they add a lot of pairwise work for details that are difficult to notice at the size the particles are drawn. The goal was to preserve the visible consequences of the physics—momentum, falling, drag, and settling—without turning the page into a full rigid-body or soft-body simulator.

Numerical integration

The physics runs at a fixed timestep of 1/120 s. For each step, acceleration updates velocity first, and the new velocity then updates position. This is semi-implicit Euler integration: still very inexpensive, but generally better behaved for spring-like systems than updating position from the old velocity.

Rendering is kept separate from the physics clock. A 60 Hz display, a 144 Hz display, or an occasional slow frame should therefore not change the underlying motion very much. If rendering falls behind, the simulation performs multiple fixed physics steps to catch up, but I cap accumulated catch-up time at 0.1 s. Without that cap, one unusually slow frame can create a feedback loop where the browser has to run more and more physics just to catch up.

This follows the fixed-timestep approach in Fix Your Timestep! ↗. Fiedler’s Integration Basics ↗ is also a good reference for the integration method.

Collisions and interactions

Not everything on screen needs detailed geometry. Trees use circular footprints for collision checks, which is enough to make the dog steer around their trunks without testing against every transparent pixel of the sprite. The four project objects use simple proximity checks: reaching one counts as an encounter and opens its project card. Grounding shadows are just drawn ellipses rather than simulated lighting.

These approximations are intentional. I tried to use detailed physics where it changes how the scene feels and cheap geometric tests where the exact result is visually indistinguishable.

Performance tradeoffs

Grass was the main performance constraint because there can be many tufts on screen at once. The terrain and small grass sprites are cached instead of rebuilt every frame. Rooted grass is also sorted once when the field is generated; each frame only has to merge that mostly static ordering with the small number of objects that actually move.

The loose-particle system is capped at 320 particles. This gives enough density for a visible burst without allowing repeated movement to create an ever-growing simulation. More importantly, particles do not collide with one another. Pairwise collision detection would move the cost toward quadratic growth as the particle count increases, while contributing very little visually for tiny pieces of grass.

The same reasoning is why a tuft gets one bend state instead of a simulation for every blade. There is a lot of physics I could add, but realism was not the only objective. I wanted the interactions that matter visually—acceleration, inertia, spring recovery, gravity, drag, and collision avoidance—while keeping the amount of state and computation small enough for an interactive webpage.

What isn’t simulated

This is still a deliberately stylized model. There is no airflow, blade-by-blade grass mechanics, particle-particle collision, leaf deformation, articulated dog skeleton, or physical lighting model. Trees are placed procedurally around the edges of the scene rather than grown from a terrain model. Some effects are also exaggerated because physically accurate motion can be too subtle to read at this scale.

I think of the simulation as a perceptual model rather than a complete physical one: spend computation on the dynamics a person notices, and approximate or omit the ones they probably won’t.
