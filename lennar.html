Analysis / Homebuilding

Lennar (LEN)

Lower capital employed has not yet translated into higher financial returns.

September 21, 2026

I would buy at $65 before recovery, or pay more after margins and cash flow improve.

The business

Lennar builds and sells homes. It increasingly uses land banks, which finance land acquisition and development while Lennar pays deposits for the right to buy finished lots. In February 2025, Lennar spun off Millrose Properties as a separate land provider, transferring approximately $5.6 billion of land and $1.0 billion of cash. From November 2024 to November 2025, owned homesites declined from 85,428 to 9,525. [1]

This reduces the capital Lennar commits before construction. It also increases finished-lot costs because land banks must earn a return. Management expects faster asset turnover to offset lower margins and increase return on equity (ROE). The relevant test is whether Lennar earns more on the capital it retains, not how little land it owns.

The results

In the quarter ended August 31, 2026, Lennar reported a 116-day construction cycle and construction costs per square foot 14% below the fourth quarter of 2023. Home-sale gross margin nevertheless fell from 17.5% to 15.8% year over year. Management attributed the decline to lower revenue per square foot and higher land costs, partly offset by construction savings. [2]

Fiscal 2025 operating cash flow was $217 million, compared with more than $2 billion of net income. Deposits and pre-acquisition costs absorbed approximately $1.55 billion of operating cash flow. Lennar has reduced owned land, but deposits still require substantial capital. The new model has not yet demonstrated strong cash conversion. [1]

D.R. Horton reported a 20.4% home-sales gross margin for the nine months ended June 30, 2026. The reporting period, geographic mix, and land ownership differ from Lennar's. Horton is therefore a benchmark, not a controlled comparison or a recommendation to buy its stock. Its results show that another large builder is earning substantially higher margins under similar housing conditions. [3]

What I would pay

At the September 18 closing price of $76.43, Lennar offers little discount to my base estimate of $79. I would not buy at that price on the current evidence. My purchase limits are $65 before margin recovery, $75 with improved margins and cash conversion, and $85 if the business demonstrates stronger returns. [4]

The valuation uses estimated book value of $90.68 per share, an 11% required annual return, and 3% long-term growth. Book value uses August 31 equity and quarterly diluted average shares, rather than period-end shares. The return and growth rates are my assumptions. [2]

Estimated value = book value × (sustainable ROE − growth) ÷ (required return − growth). Sustainable ROE is the return Lennar earns on its equity through a normal housing cycle. The required return is what I demand from owning the stock.

The earnings calculation assumes $30 billion of annual home-sale revenue, $425 million of Financial Services pretax earnings, $650 million of corporate expense, no net earnings from other businesses, and a 25% tax rate. With 18.5% gross margin and SG&A at 8% of home-sale revenue, that produces $2.19 billion of net income, or 10.2% on $21.56 billion of equity. At 19% gross margin and 8% SG&A, earnings rise to $2.31 billion and ROE to 10.7%. At 20% gross margin and 7.5% SG&A, earnings reach $2.64 billion and ROE 12.3%. The valuation table uses rounded 10%, 11%, and 12% scenarios, not exact forecasts.

An investor requiring only 10% annually would value the 10% ROE case at about $91. I use 11% because the margin recovery and cash generation remain unproven. The model does not separately forecast the years required to recover; the purchase discounts provide some allowance for that uncertainty.

The conditions for buying

$65 before recovery

The third-quarter 10-Q needs to show where cash is being absorbed. I would compare year-to-date deposit additions, mandatory lot purchases, and option losses with the same period of 2025. If those figures do not undermine the 10% normalized ROE case, my purchase limit is $65. This is an investment in a recovery that has not yet occurred. If sustainable ROE instead settles at 8%, estimated value falls to $57. The $65 limit is not a downside floor.

$75 after margins and cash flow improve

I would raise the limit to $75 after two consecutive quarters with home-sale gross margin at least 19%, SG&A no higher than 8% of home-sale revenue, and inventory turns above 2×. Trailing-year operating cash flow must also reach at least 80% of net income. At the modeled $2.31 billion of earnings, that means approximately $1.85 billion of operating cash flow. These are my purchase criteria, not management guidance.

I would not count cash released by shrinking inventory as evidence of better recurring cash generation. The cash-flow statement needs to show that earnings are converting to cash without reducing the scale of the business. Higher margins alone are insufficient if deposits continue absorbing the additional profit.

$85 with stronger returns

I would raise the limit to $85 after two consecutive quarters with gross margin at least 20% and SG&A no higher than 7.5%. Inventory turns must remain above 2× and trailing-year cash conversion at least 80%, without relying on inventory liquidation. At modeled earnings of $2.64 billion, the cash-flow requirement is approximately $2.12 billion. Those results would support the 12% ROE case and a value near $102.

Conclusion

I would buy at $65 under the base recovery case, but not at $76.43 on today's results. I would pay $75 or $85 only after Lennar meets the corresponding margin and cash-flow tests. All three limits assume revenue capacity and book value remain intact; a decline in either requires a new valuation.

Lennar guides to 15.5–16.0% fourth-quarter gross margin. Meeting that guidance would not raise my purchase limit. The fourth-quarter earnings release and fiscal 2026 10-K are the next annual check, not a deadline for recovery. The reason to pay more is higher earnings supported by cash flow, not a higher share price. [2]
