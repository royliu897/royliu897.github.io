Analysis / Homebuilding

Lennar (LEN)

A land-light homebuilder trading near tangible book with an unproven operating model.

September 21, 2026

Verdict: pass at ~$76. Revisit below $65, not an automatic buy.

The short version

Lennar is the second-largest U.S. homebuilder. Over the past five years it has almost completely externalized its land ownership — from 61% owned in 2020 to 2% today — by shifting to option-based land access through Millrose Properties and other land banks. The idea is straightforward: own less land, turn capital faster, earn higher returns on equity even at lower margins per home.

The operational side of this transformation is real. Owned homesites fell from ~174,000 in 2018 to 9,525 by November 2025. Inventory turns rose from ~1.0× to ~2.5×. Construction cost per square foot dropped ~14% from Q4 2023 to Q3 2026. Build cycle times are down to ~116 days. These are not projections — they have already happened.

The financial side has not followed. Home-sale gross margin fell from 22.3% in 2024 to 17.7% in 2025 to 15.8% in Q3 2026. Net income dropped 47% from 2024 to 2025. Operating cash flow collapsed from $5.2B in 2023 to $217M in 2025, largely because real-estate deposits ballooned from $3.5B to $6.3B as Lennar replaced owned land with option commitments. By August 2026, deposits had reached ~$7.3B.

The bull case is that this is cyclical. Mortgage rates near 7%, 9.6 months of new-home supply, and incentives running at ~12% of revenue are compressing margins industry-wide. When rates fall and affordability improves, margins recover, and the higher asset turnover produces better returns on less capital.

The problem is D.R. Horton. Horton operates in the same macro environment with a similar (though less extreme) land-light model — ~78% controlled versus Lennar's 98% — and is earning a home-sales gross margin of approximately 20.4% over the nine months ended June 2026, versus Lennar's 15.8% in the quarter ended August 2026. Those are different reporting windows, not a controlled experiment. Geography, product mix, production strategy, and Horton's ownership of Forestar also differ. The comparison challenges a purely cyclical explanation; it does not prove that Lennar's land-light model is structurally inferior.

What the price implies

At ~$76, Lennar trades at roughly tangible book value (~$76/share) and ~0.84× reported book (~$91/share). Using a residual-income framework with an 11–12% required return and 3% long-term growth, the current price implies the market expects ~10% normalized ROE. That's not unreasonable — it sits between the base case (~18.5% gross margin, ~10% ROE) and the weak case (~17.5%, ~8–9% ROE). But it's not cheap either. You're paying roughly fair value for a business that hasn't demonstrated it can earn that return under the new model.

The stock gets interesting around $60–65. At 0.7× book, you need only high-single-digit normalized ROE to earn an acceptable return — a much less demanding bet. At $76, you need to believe both that margins recover to ~18.5% and that deposit growth stabilizes. Neither has been shown yet.

What I'm watching

Gross margin is the single most important number. A sustained move toward 18–19% without a return to pandemic-era housing conditions would validate the model. Margin stuck at 15–17% after affordability improves would suggest the weakness is structural.

Deposit growth matters almost as much. The whole point of asset-light is less working capital per dollar of revenue. If deposits keep growing faster than homebuilding activity, the capital Lennar removed from owned land is just reappearing elsewhere.

Operating cash flow should converge toward earnings once the deposit build stabilizes. Two years of earnings substantially exceeding cash flow is not yet a red flag — the transition is still in progress — but it cannot persist indefinitely.

D.R. Horton remains a useful benchmark. If the margin gap narrows, Lennar's model is working. If Horton continues earning 20%+ gross margins while Lennar stays in the mid-teens, the argument for 98% controlled land over 78% becomes very hard to make.

Bottom line

Lennar is not broken. The balance sheet is conservative, the land exposure is dramatically reduced, and the construction operations are genuinely improving. But "not broken" and "good investment at this price" are different claims. At $76, I need to see margin recovery and deposit stabilization before the risk/reward makes sense. Below $65, the margin of safety does more of the work and the required assumptions get easier.

My conclusion is a pass at the price analyzed, not a prediction that Lennar is broken. Operational improvements are not enough if shareholders still earn inadequate returns. Below $65, I would revisit the assumptions rather than buy mechanically: deteriorating book value or structurally weaker returns could erase the apparent discount.
