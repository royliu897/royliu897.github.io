Analysis / Homebuilding

Lennar (LEN)

Housing recovery is only part of the investment case.

September 21, 2026

I expect Lennar to emerge from the housing downturn able to build more homes with less capital per home through its land-light operating model.

I would buy below $80 with a five-year horizon. My successful-transition case supports $150–180 per share, with further upside from stronger growth.

Why is Lennar interesting?

Lennar first caught my attention because it was trading below book value and Berkshire Hathaway was building its position after selling competitor D.R. Horton. Berkshire exited its reported Horton holding in Q3 2025. Its Lennar Class A holding then grew from 7.05 million shares that September to 13.11 million by June 2026, as Lennar's quarter-end share price fell. To me, those purchases suggest Berkshire sees the weakness as a chance to buy a promising business at a discount. [3]

At $76.43 on September 18, Lennar traded at about 0.84 times estimated book value. I wanted to understand what the price was missing. My interest is now less in the discount to book and more in what Lennar could earn with a more efficient operating model. [1] [2]

What does Lennar do?

Lennar builds and sells homes and provides mortgage and closing services. Land banks finance land acquisition and development. Lennar pays for purchase options and buys finished lots as construction requires them. Millrose Properties, spun off in February 2025, is one of those providers. Lennar transferred approximately $5.6 billion of land and $1.0 billion of cash in the separation. [4]

Paying deposits and recurring fees lets Lennar avoid funding years of land ownership before construction begins. It combines this with even-flow production, keeping construction starts aligned with sales so subcontractors have a predictable workload. The aim is to build homes at lower cost with less capital tied up along the way.

How is Lennar doing?

Lennar has maintained volume by accepting lower prices and margins. Deliveries rose from 80,210 homes in 2024 to 82,583 in 2025, while average selling price fell from $423,000 to $391,000. Home-sale revenue declined from $33.8 billion to $32.1 billion, and gross margin fell from 22.3% to 17.7%. Net earnings declined from $3.93 billion to $2.08 billion. [4]

In Q3 2026, deliveries fell 3% year over year to 20,840 homes and orders fell 9%. Gross margin declined to 15.8%, while SG&A reached 9.2% of home-sale revenue, leaving a 6.6% home-sale operating margin. Net income fell from $591 million to $284 million. Earnings have deteriorated much faster than delivery volume. [2]

High mortgage rates limit buyers' purchasing power. Lennar uses price reductions and financing incentives to maintain sales. Management reported approximately 12% incentives in Q3 2026, down from 14.5% in Q4 2025 but still well above its historical 4–6% range. Regional pressure has been greater. In Q3 2025, reported incentives reached 20.5% in South Central and 17.7% in the East, compared with 10.5% in the West. Lower revenue per square foot and higher land costs have compressed margins despite construction savings. [2] [5] [6] [9]

Land costs explain part of the gap between better construction and weaker margins. Management reports construction cost per square foot down approximately 14% since Q4 2023, while land cost per homesite has risen approximately 6%. It attributes the pressure to lots committed under stronger market conditions and higher accumulated option-maintenance fees. Each closing moves an older lot through cost of sales. Replacing those lots with land underwritten at current selling prices is a second source of margin recovery, separate from lower incentives. [15]

Construction performance has improved. Q3 cycle time reached 116 days, construction cost per square foot fell 6% year over year, and inventory turns reached 2.4×. Completed unsold inventory declined from 2.1 to 1.8 homes per community during the quarter. These improvements reduce the cost and capital required to build a home. They have not yet offset weak selling economics. [2]

Looking back to 2018 gives a better sense of the change. Land-related investment, including deposits and homebuilding joint ventures, remained near $9 billion from 2018 to 2025. Including finished homes and construction in progress, the total was approximately $17.9 billion in both years. Over that period, annual deliveries rose from 48,856, including a full year of the acquired builder CalAtlantic, to 82,583. On my calculation, investment per annual delivery fell from approximately $365,500 to $216,100, a 41% reduction. Lennar now supports materially more production with roughly the same balance-sheet investment. [4] [14]

Cash conversion remains weak. Operating cash flow fell from $2.40 billion in 2024 to $217 million in 2025. Deposits and pre-acquisition costs absorbed approximately $1.55 billion of operating cash in 2025. By Q3 2026, the combined balance reached $7.3 billion, up $265 million sequentially. Deposits themselves were flat. The increase mainly represented municipal reimbursements receivable for completed development, with a smaller increase in capitalized option-maintenance fees. [4] [15]

Deposits and option fees are paid before lots are purchased and homes are sold. That timing helps explain why cash flow lags during the transition. Over time, I expect less capital per delivery to leave more cash after funding replacement lots and growth. [9]

At August 2026, homebuilding debt was $4.30 billion against $1.15 billion of cash. Debt represented 16.6% of homebuilding capital. Leverage remains moderate, but net homebuilding debt rose from $643 million in November 2025 to $3.15 billion. [2] The Millrose separation also matters when comparing book value across years. Assets were distributed to shareholders rather than simply lost from the business. [4]

Thesis

I think the market already expects Lennar's margins to recover. What interests me is what happens after that. Lennar would be selling into a better market with shorter build times, lower construction costs and much less capital committed to each delivery. I expect that combination to let it grow faster and return more cash to shareholders than it could under its old model.

Horton demonstrates that controlled land can support a large business with strong returns. Its homebuilding pretax return on inventory increased from 12.8% in 2015 to 18.1% in 2019. By September 2019, it controlled 60% of its lots through purchase contracts and earned 17.2% ROE. Those results predate the pandemic boom. Land strategy was not the only contributor, but the results support Lennar's approach. [7] [8]

NVR, another large homebuilder, has taken land-light building further. At December 2025, it controlled 169,250 lots through third-party purchase agreements, backed by approximately $925 million of cash deposits and letters of credit. It directly owned land expected to produce about 2,300 lots and also held joint-venture interests. It earned a 21.2% homebuilding gross margin in 2025. Near-total reliance on outside land developers is compatible with strong margins, although NVR's contracts and market mix differ from Lennar's. [11]

I see no reason to treat Horton's ownership mix as the limit. Lennar can commit less to each site and spread its land investment across more communities. Its scale also makes it a substantial, repeat customer for land providers. I expect those advantages to outweigh the fees, with land-related investment growing more slowly than the deliveries it supports.

Lennar's community count increased from 1,447 at the end of 2024 to 1,708 at the end of 2025, an 18% increase. Some expansion came through the acquisition of regional builder Rausch Coleman Homes, where Lennar bought the homebuilding business and Millrose bought the land. The transaction expanded Lennar into new markets without requiring it to purchase the associated land outright. [4]

Share gains are already visible in Dallas–Fort Worth. From 2023 to 2025, Lennar's annual closings increased from 4,857 to 5,724 and its market share rose from 11.3% to 14.1%. Horton's share fell from 19.2% to 17.3%. Lennar narrowed the gap while increasing its own volume. The next step is turning that stronger position into higher profit per home. [16]

More communities give Lennar greater choice about where to build and allow it to spread production across more locations. Steady work lets subcontractors plan their crews, while larger purchases improve Lennar's bargaining power on materials. Lower costs and shorter build times then leave more cash for additional communities or repurchases. I expect these advantages to reinforce each other as Lennar grows.

My successful case is 100,000 annual deliveries at around 20% home-sale gross margin, without a proportionate increase in equity. Becoming the largest builder matters less to me than the earnings and cash each share would represent.

Land options are not proprietary. Horton and NVR already use them, and competitors can expand their use. Lennar's advantage has to come from combining scale, local execution, predictable production, and access to land capital better than its competitors.

Management's incentives point in the same direction. The 2026 proxy describes CEO Stuart Miller's fiscal 2025 cash bonus as a share of adjusted pretax income after a 7.3% charge on tangible capital, defined as equity less intangibles plus homebuilding debt. That rewards earnings relative to capital rather than revenue growth alone. The calculation excludes impairment charges, so it is not a complete measure of the cost of poor investment decisions. [12]

Potential Areas of Concern

Demand is the largest risk to the growth thesis. The remaining concerns are mainly trade-offs between external land financing and direct ownership. Their significance depends on how much capital and flexibility Lennar gains in exchange.

  • Demand and production pace. If buyers cannot absorb production at adequate margins, Lennar must increase incentives or reduce starts. That gives up either profit per home or some of the cost advantages of steady construction. Temporary weakness is manageable. Persistent discounting would undermine the growth thesis, especially if unsold inventory rises despite lower prices.

  • Land economics. Lots committed under earlier pricing assumptions can remain expensive relative to what buyers will pay today. New purchases can reflect current conditions, but longer holding periods also accumulate more option fees. Lower incentives will not solve that cost pressure by themselves. The comparison is with the full cost of owning land, not with free financing. Failure to improve profit per home as pricing and the land pipeline reset would be evidence against the thesis.

  • Cash conversion. Deposits and capitalized fees can absorb cash well before delivery. Smaller commitments should ultimately support more homes than outright ownership, but the benefit must appear in cash available after reinvestment. If rising deposits, fees, or purchase obligations continually consume the earnings, the expected shareholder returns will not follow.

  • Growth quality. More communities and market share only create value if they produce profitable deliveries without proportionate increases in capital. Provider delays, excessive land commitments, or sustained borrowing to fund growth and buybacks would weaken that outcome. Lower capital intensity should make expansion easier to finance, not justify expansion at any price.

Value

I start with what Lennar could look like around 2031. At 100,000 deliveries and a $390,000 average selling price, home-sale revenue would be $39 billion. Reaching that volume from 82,583 deliveries in 2025 requires about 3.9% annual unit growth over five years. The required growth is slightly lower over the six fiscal years from 2025 to 2031.

Community count provides another check. At the Q3 2026 sales pace of 4.1 homes per community per month, 100,000 annual sales would require about 2,033 active communities on average, 19% above the 1,708 at year-end 2025. Converting that into 100,000 deliveries assumes sales translate into closings over time. The scenario requires more communities, but not faster sales at each one. New communities still need enough local demand to sustain that pace. [15] [4]

At 20% gross margin and 8% SG&A, home-sale operating profit would be $4.68 billion. I add $425 million of Financial Services pretax earnings, subtract $650 million for corporate and other net costs, and apply a 25% tax rate. That leaves approximately $3.34 billion of annual net income. These are my assumptions for a successful transition, including land-bank costs within gross margin.

At 220 million shares, earnings would be about $15.19 per share. I use 10–12 times earnings for the main valuation range. That gives credit for a more efficient business while still treating homebuilding as cyclical. A 14-times multiple is an upside case that requires stronger confidence in durable growth and cash returns.

All three cases use the same Financial Services contribution, corporate and other net costs, and tax rate. The successful case assumes the share count falls from roughly 240 million to 220 million. At an average repurchase price of $100, that would require approximately $2 billion over five years, after funding growth and dividends. Without those buybacks, the same earnings on 240 million shares would support approximately $139–167 at 10–12 times earnings.

The successful case earns about 34% more per share than ordinary recovery, without needing a higher valuation multiple. Increasing deliveries from 90,000 to 100,000 takes EPS from $11.36 to $12.70. One additional point of gross margin brings it to $13.92, and the lower share count brings it to $15.19. This is where I see the value of the transition, in the combination of growth, better margins and repurchases rather than any one assumption alone.

At 14 times $15.19 of earnings, the successful case reaches approximately $213. Relative to $76.43, the $152–213 range is about 2.0–2.8 times the entry price. A full tripling would require roughly $229, or about 15 times those earnings. I see doubling as a credible success outcome. Tripling requires more earnings or a higher valuation than my main case.

These are future prices, not estimates of today's fair value. Discounting $152–182 back five years at a 12% annual return gives roughly $86–103 before dividends. That is why the current price interests me, but the weak-demand case is a real loss scenario. The investment rests on my confidence in the transition, not on treating ordinary recovery as a guaranteed downside limit.

Action

  • I would buy below $80. My main success range is $150–180 by roughly September 2031. From an $80 entry, that is about 13.4–17.6% annualized price appreciation. From $76.43, it is about 14.4–18.7%, before dividends and taxes.
  • I would reassess around $150–180. I would sell if the price reflected the successful transition without enough further earnings growth. I would not sell at $85 simply because the stock had recovered.
  • I would sell earlier if the thesis breaks. The clearest signs would be persistent weak cash conversion, rising capital required per delivery, or failure to improve profit per home as pricing recovers.

There are two timing questions. Housing affordability determines how much pricing Lennar can retain. Separately, land commitments made years before a home sells take time to pass through earnings. Millrose reported a 3.5-year weighted-average remaining life for its Lennar master-program portfolio at March 2026. That shows the pipeline spans several years, not when Lennar's margins will recover. [13]

I use five years to allow both processes to develop. Fannie Mae forecasts new-home sales rising from 630,000 in 2026 to 668,000 in 2027, with mortgage rates averaging 6.7% in 2027. The case does not depend on identifying the exact housing bottom or assuming newly purchased land is automatically cheaper. It depends on retaining operating gains and earning better returns as the business works through its existing commitments. [10]
