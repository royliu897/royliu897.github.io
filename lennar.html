Analysis / Homebuilding

Lennar (LEN)

Housing recovery is only part of the investment case.

September 21, 2026

I expect housing recovery to restore earnings and the land-light model to let Lennar grow with less capital. The second part is why I am interested.

I would buy below $80 with a five-year horizon. My successful-transition case supports $150–180 per share, with further upside from stronger growth.

Why is Lennar interesting?

Lennar first caught my attention because it was trading below book value and Berkshire was building its position after selling Horton. Berkshire exited its reported Horton holding in Q3 2025. Its Lennar Class A holding then grew from 7.05 million shares that September to 13.11 million by June 2026, as Lennar's quarter-end share price fell. To me, those purchases suggest Berkshire sees the weakness as a chance to buy a promising business at a discount. [3]

At $76.43 on September 18, Lennar traded at about 0.84 times estimated book value. I wanted to understand what the price was missing. My interest is now less in the discount to book and more in what Lennar could earn with a more efficient operating model. [1] [2]

What does Lennar do?

Lennar builds and sells homes and provides mortgage and closing services. Land banks finance land acquisition and development. Lennar pays for purchase options and buys finished lots as construction requires them. Millrose Properties, spun off in February 2025, is one of those providers. Lennar transferred approximately $5.6 billion of land and $1.0 billion of cash in the separation. [4]

This substitutes deposits and recurring fees for much of the capital required to own a land pipeline. Lennar also seeks to keep construction starts aligned with sales. Predictable work can improve subcontractor scheduling and purchasing economics. The intended result is less capital and lower construction cost per delivery, not independence from housing demand.

How is Lennar doing?

Lennar has maintained volume by accepting lower prices and margins. Deliveries rose from 80,210 homes in 2024 to 82,583 in 2025, while average selling price fell from $423,000 to $391,000. Home-sale revenue declined from $33.8 billion to $32.1 billion, and gross margin fell from 22.3% to 17.7%. Net earnings declined from $3.93 billion to $2.08 billion. [4]

In Q3 2026, deliveries fell 3% year over year to 20,840 homes and orders fell 9%. Gross margin declined to 15.8%, while SG&A reached 9.2% of home-sale revenue, leaving a 6.6% home-sale operating margin. Net income fell from $591 million to $284 million. Earnings have deteriorated much faster than delivery volume. [2]

High mortgage rates limit buyers' purchasing power. Lennar uses price reductions and financing incentives to maintain sales. Management reported approximately 12% incentives in Q3 2026, down from 14.5% in Q4 2025 but still well above its historical 4–6% range. Regional pressure has been greater. In Q3 2025, reported incentives reached 20.5% in South Central and 17.7% in the East, compared with 10.5% in the West. Lower revenue per square foot and higher land costs have compressed margins despite construction savings. [2] [5] [6] [9]

Construction performance has improved. Q3 cycle time reached 116 days, construction cost per square foot fell 6% year over year, and inventory turns reached 2.4×. Completed unsold inventory declined from 2.1 to 1.8 homes per community during the quarter. These improvements reduce the cost and capital required to build a home. They have not yet offset weak selling economics. [2]

Cash conversion remains weak. Operating cash flow fell from $2.40 billion in 2024 to $217 million in 2025. Deposits and pre-acquisition costs absorbed approximately $1.55 billion of operating cash in 2025. On the Q2 2026 call, the CFO reported a further $237 million sequential increase in the combined balance to $7.1 billion. Deposits were flat, with the increase primarily coming from capitalized option-maintenance fees. [4] [9]

At August 2026, homebuilding debt was $4.30 billion against $1.15 billion of cash. Debt represented 16.6% of homebuilding capital. Leverage remains moderate, but net homebuilding debt rose from $643 million in November 2025 to $3.15 billion. [2] The Millrose separation also matters when comparing book value across years. Assets were distributed to shareholders rather than simply lost from the business. [4]

Thesis

I believe Lennar will emerge from the housing downturn with a more capital-efficient business. Margin recovery should restore part of its earnings. The additional value comes from retaining lower construction costs and shorter build times while committing less capital to land. That would allow Lennar to support more sales with each dollar of equity, or return capital without reducing sales.

Horton demonstrates that controlled land can support a large business with strong returns. Its homebuilding pretax return on inventory increased from 12.8% in 2015 to 18.1% in 2019. By September 2019, it controlled 60% of its lots through purchase contracts and earned 17.2% ROE. Those results predate the pandemic boom. Land strategy was not the only contributor, but Horton validates the direction Lennar is taking. It does not set a ceiling on how far Lennar should go. [7] [8]

I do not consider Horton's ownership mix an optimal limit. Lennar's scale and predictable production should help it use external land capital efficiently. Smaller commitments per site also allow a broader land pipeline and less exposure to any single project. I expect those benefits to outweigh the additional fees, as long as total land-related capital grows more slowly than the productive capacity it supports. That capacity ultimately needs to translate into profitable deliveries, not just a larger count of controlled lots.

I think the market already expects some margin recovery. What I believe it underestimates is Lennar's capacity to grow after that recovery. Less capital tied up in each land position lets the same equity support more communities. A broader pipeline gives Lennar more choice about where to build. If sales support steady production, subcontractors can plan crews more efficiently and Lennar can buy materials at greater scale. Lower costs and shorter construction cycles then free cash for the next set of communities. This is how I expect the operating gains to compound. Lennar becomes a better competitor, not just a recovered builder.

I am looking for a business capable of delivering 100,000 homes annually, earning around 20% home-sale gross margin, and funding growth without a proportionate increase in equity. Becoming the largest builder would be a consequence of that progress. The value comes from earnings and cash per share, not the ranking.

Potential Areas of Concern

Demand is the largest risk to the growth thesis. The remaining concerns are mainly trade-offs between external land financing and direct ownership. Their significance depends on how much capital and flexibility Lennar gains in exchange.

  • Demand and production pace. If buyers cannot absorb production at adequate margins, Lennar must increase incentives or reduce starts, weakening either profitability or the cost advantages of steady construction. A temporary slowdown would not invalidate land-light ownership, but persistent discounting would undermine the growth thesis. Higher gross profit per delivery without a buildup of unsold homes would show that operating efficiency is translating into better financial results.

  • Land-bank costs. Maintenance fees accumulate before lots are purchased and later enter land basis, reducing the margin retained on each home. [9] Direct ownership also incurs carrying costs and ties up capital for years. Paying an external provider can improve returns despite lower margins if the capital savings and reduced land exposure outweigh the fees.

  • Deposits and cash conversion. Expanding the controlled-land pipeline absorbs cash through deposits and pre-acquisition costs, leaving less available for shareholder returns. A rising balance is not itself evidence of deteriorating economics, since those commitments can support more homesites than the same capital invested in outright ownership. The relevant measure is total land-related capital relative to the profitable deliveries it supports.

  • Land access and contractual obligations. Reliance on outside providers creates exposure to financing and development delays, while purchase commitments can restrict Lennar's ability to exit weaker projects. [4] Owning land avoids provider dependence but concentrates more capital in individual parcels and does not eliminate development risk. Smaller option commitments offer greater flexibility where the contracts preserve the ability to withdraw at an acceptable cost.

  • Growth and capital allocation. Community expansion and buybacks compete for cash, so the lower share count assumed in the valuation requires earnings to convert into cash after funding growth. Lower capital requirements per home should make both easier to support. Sustained increases in borrowing would instead suggest that the expected cash benefit has not materialized.

Value

I start with what Lennar could look like around 2031. At 100,000 deliveries and a $390,000 average selling price, home-sale revenue would be $39 billion. Reaching that volume from 82,583 deliveries in 2025 requires about 3.9% annual unit growth over five years. The required growth is slightly lower over the six fiscal years from 2025 to 2031.

At 20% gross margin and 8% SG&A, home-sale operating profit would be $4.68 billion. I add $425 million of Financial Services pretax earnings, subtract $650 million for corporate and other net costs, and apply a 25% tax rate. That leaves approximately $3.34 billion of annual net income. These are my assumptions for a successful transition, including land-bank costs within gross margin.

At 220 million shares, earnings would be about $15.19 per share. I use 10–12 times earnings for the main valuation range. That gives credit for a more efficient business while still treating homebuilding as cyclical. A 14-times multiple is an upside case that requires stronger confidence in durable growth and cash returns.

All three cases use the same Financial Services contribution, corporate and other net costs, and tax rate. The successful case assumes the share count falls from roughly 240 million to 220 million. At an average repurchase price of $100, that would require approximately $2 billion over five years, after funding growth and dividends. Without those buybacks, the same earnings on 240 million shares would support approximately $139–167 at 10–12 times earnings.

This is where I see the potential value of land-light building. Lennar needs to fund more deliveries while freeing enough cash for shareholders. Higher volume alone would not demonstrate that advantage. The share-count assumption depends on cash conversion, and the margin assumption depends on retaining pricing recovery after paying land providers.

At 14 times $15.19 of earnings, the successful case reaches approximately $213. Relative to $76.43, the $152–213 range is about 2.0–2.8 times the entry price. A full tripling would require roughly $229, or about 15 times those earnings. I see doubling as a credible success outcome. Tripling requires more earnings or a higher valuation than my main case.

These are future prices, not estimates of today's fair value. Discounting $152–182 back five years at a 12% annual return gives roughly $86–103 before dividends. That is why the current price interests me, but the weak-demand case is a real loss scenario. The investment rests on my confidence in the transition, not on treating ordinary recovery as a guaranteed downside limit.

Action

  • I would buy below $80. My main success range is $150–180 by roughly September 2031. From an $80 entry, that is about 13.4–17.6% annualized price appreciation. From $76.43, it is about 14.4–18.7%, before dividends and taxes.
  • I would reassess around $150–180. I would sell if the price reflected the successful transition without enough further earnings growth. I would not sell at $85 simply because the stock had recovered.
  • I would sell earlier if the thesis breaks. The clearest signs would be persistent weak cash conversion, rising capital required per delivery, or failure to improve profit per home as pricing recovers.

I use five years because housing conditions will take time to improve. Fannie Mae forecasts new-home sales rising from 630,000 in 2026 to 668,000 in 2027, with mortgage rates averaging 6.7% in 2027. I expect gradual improvement rather than a return to cheap mortgages. The investment does not require a precise call on the first quarter of recovery. It requires Lennar to retain its operating gains when demand improves. [10]
